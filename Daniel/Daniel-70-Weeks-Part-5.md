### Apostolic Evangelism Patterns

- The early apostles, including Paul, focused their ministry on Jewish populations throughout the Roman world (the diaspora)
    - This included regions such as modern-day Turkey, Cyprus, Greece, and Macedonia
- **Missionary Strategy**
    - Upon arriving in a new town, the first point of contact was always the local synagogue
    - They would often attend Shabbat services for several consecutive weeks
- **Conflict with Synagogue Leadership**
    - The goal of visiting was to lead many Jewish people to faith in Jesus
    - This often led to the apostles being ejected or excommunicated from the synagogue, frequently due to jealousy from local leaders

### Chronology and Timing of the 70 Weeks

- This session serves as part 2 of the bonus study regarding the timing of the 70 weeks prophecy in Daniel 9

### Transition to Daniel Chapters 10-12

- The study will soon move into the final three chapters of Daniel
    - Chapters 10, 11, and 12 constitute one continuous prophecy
    - The central focus of this sequence is the prophecies concerning the kings of the North and South

### Historical Timeline of the 70 Weeks

- **[Review of Weeks 1–69]**
    - The timeline begins with a decree that initiates the 70 weeks
    - The first 7 weeks (49 years) cover the period from the decree to the full construction of the city of Jerusalem (including its streets and plaza)
    - There are 483 years from the beginning of that decree until the arrival of the "Messiah the Prince" as described in Daniel 9

### The Starting Point of the Prophecy

- **The Initiating Decree**
    - The prophecy is triggered by a decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem
    - The most likely historical candidate is the decree from the Persian king (referenced in Nehemiah) to reconstruct the city's walls
    - This event initiated the reconstruction and rehabilitation of Jerusalem in the decades that followed
- **Connecting the Timeline**
    - The city was fairly inhabited by the first century (the time of Jesus)
    - This period occurs approximately 483 years after the initial decree
    - Based on this chronology, the timeline places the crucifixion and resurrection of Jesus in 30 AD

### Differing Theological Perspectives on the 70th Week

- There are varying views on how to interpret the 70th week of the prophecy:
    - **Pre-millennial view**
        - Believes the 70th week is a future event yet to occur
    - **Amillennial and Post-millennial views**
        - These perspectives generally hold different interpretations of the 70th week
        - The speaker notes that these views are rational and have been widely accepted by various sects of the church throughout history

- **[Objective]** Re-evaluating these verses to understand:
    - How post-millennial interpreters view the 70th week
    - Why those views may conflict with the literal language of the text
    - How they attempt to fit the 70th week into a first-century timeline

### Messianic Figures in Daniel 9:25-27

- **The Identity of the Messiah**
    - Most interpreters agree that the *Mashiach Nagid* (Messiah the Prince) mentioned in verse 25 is Jesus
- **Alternative Interpretations**
    - Some traditions, including modern Judaism, suggest there may be two distinct messianic figures in this passage
    - One figure appears after the first seven weeks, and another appears after the sixty-two weeks
    - This interpretation is reflected in certain translations, such as the ESV, which follows the Jewish Publication Society (JPS) translation of the Hebrew scriptures

### Interpretive Perspectives on the Identity of "He"

- **Consensus on the Messiah**
    - There is almost universal agreement among Christians that the "Messiah the Prince" appearing in the 69-week timeline is Jesus
    - This identification is supported by the similar language used in Isaiah 53 regarding one who is "cut off"
- **The Primary Debate: The "Prince Who Is to Come"**
    - The central point of disagreement is whether this figure is:
        - The same as the Messiah mentioned in the previous verse
        - A different, separate prince
- **Cross-Reference with Other Prophecies**
    - The character of this figure can be compared to other entities described in the wider context of Daniel (Chapters 8, 11, and 12):
        - The "little horn"
        - The "king of the north"
    - These figures are characterized by similar actions, such as:
        - Bringing an end to the daily sacrifice on the Temple Mount
        - Defiling the Temple
        - Setting up an abomination

### The Tale of Two Princes

- **Contrasting Identities**
    - The 'prince who is to come' cannot be Jesus because Jesus does not cause desolation across the land
    - The prophecy describes a "tale of two princes":
        - **Messiah figure**: The one who is cut off (Jesus)
        - **Anti-Messiah (anti-christic) figure**: The 'prince who is to come' who causes desolation
- **The Destruction of Jerusalem**
    - The destruction of the city and the Temple is attributed to the people of this anti-christic figure
    - **Historical Context (from Josephus)**
        - The overwhelming majority (80 to 90 percent) of those recruited into the Roman army that attacked Jerusalem were provincials from Middle Eastern nations
        - These Middle Eastern men destroyed the Temple against the orders of their Roman commander, Titus

### Direct vs. Indirect Action in the Prophecy

- **[Critique of historical interpretation]** The destruction of the Temple cannot be viewed as an 'indirect' act through the Roman army
    - The language of the prophecy applies to those who *directly* committed the act of destruction
    - While divine power may be the cause behind the events, the text focuses on the direct actors

### The Covenant Confirmed by the Messiah

- **The Covenant with the Many**
    - If Jesus is the Messiah, the "covenant with the many" refers to the confirmation of the eternal covenant
    - This is the covenant made by God with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob
    - This covenant extends to all Jewish people and all the nations of the earth
- **Mechanism of Confirmation**
    - Jesus confirms this covenant through His death and resurrection

### Structural Analysis of Daniel 9:26-27

- **Methodology**
    - To understand the prophecy accurately, the verses must be broken down into individual clauses and participles
    - This prevents misinterpreting the subject of each action
- **Critique of the Jesus-as-Prince Interpretation**
    - Claiming Jesus is the "prince who is to come" (the anti-Christic figure) fails for two main reasons:
        - **Contextual Inconsistency**: It contradicts the surrounding context of other prophecies in Daniel
        - **Chronological Disruption**: It requires jumping back and forth in the historical timeline to make the events fit
- **Key Chronological Markers in the Text**

    - **The Messiah's end**: "Then after the sixty-two weeks, the Messiah will be cut off and have nothing" (AD 30-33)
    - **The destruction**: "and the people of the prince who is to come will destroy the city and the sanctuary" (AD 70)
    - **The covenant**: "And he will confirm a covenant with the many for one week"
    - **The interruption**: "but in the middle of the week he will put a stop to sacrifice and grain offering" (AD 30-33?)
    - **The desolation**: "and on the wing of abominations will come the one who makes desolate..." (AD 70)

### Chronological Tensions in Daniel 9:26-27

- **The Consensus on AD 70**
    - Most agree that the "people of the prince who is to come" destroying the city and sanctuary refers to the Roman destruction in AD 70
- **The Interpretive Conflict**
    - If Jesus is identified as the "prince to come," the timeline for the covenant and the stopping of sacrifices shifts backward to AD 30-33
    - However, if the "one who makes desolate" refers to the AD 70 destruction, it suggests a different chronological framework
- **Futurist Perspective**
    - This tension leads many futurists to argue that the 70th week is a yet-to-be-fulfilled event in the future, rather than being completed in the first century

### Chronological Flow and the Jesus Interpretation

- **The Problem of Temporal Jumps**
    - Identifying Jesus as the "prince who is to come" creates a disjointed timeline
    - Instead of a continuous progression, the interpretation requires jumping back and forth within a roughly 40-year period (the span between AD 30 and AD 70)
- **Expected Forward Progression**
    - A cohesive reading suggests a linear sequence of events:

        1. The end of the 69 weeks (Messiah is cut off)
        2. The destruction of the sanctuary by the people of the prince (roughly 4 years later)
        3. The arrival of the prince who confirms a covenant for one week
        4. The setting up of the abomination that causes desolation

    - This linear model maintains a "forward timeline," whereas the Jesus-as-Prince model creates a chronological mess.

### Critique of the Jesus-as-Prince Argument

- **Exegetical and Narrative Friction**
    - Identifying Jesus as the "prince who is to come" creates significant interpretive difficulty
    - It requires "too much gymnastics" to reconcile his crucifixion and the destruction of Jerusalem within the specific constraints of the prophecy
- **The Argument for Alignment**
    - Proponents of this view attempt to align the timeline with Jesus's ministry:
        - The 70th week is viewed as a seven-year period
        - Jesus being "cut off" (death) occurs roughly three and a half years into his ministry
        - This aligns with the prophecy's claim that the figure puts an end to sacrifice and grain offering in the middle of that seven-year week

### Inconsistencies in the Jesus-as-Prince Argument

- **The Sacrificial System Conflict**
    - Many point to the tearing of the temple curtain at Jesus' crucifixion as the "stopping of sacrifice"
    - However, the sacrificial system in the Jerusalem Temple did not actually end at that time
    - It continued for another 40 years until the Roman army destroyed the Temple in AD 70
- **Unfulfilled Prophetic Objectives**
    - If the 70th week was fulfilled during Jesus' ministry, it leaves the final three and a half years of the prophecy unaccounted for
    - The prophecy specifically aims to accomplish several key things that do not align with a first-century fulfillment:
        - Bring an end to transgression
        - Usher in everlasting righteousness
        - Seal up vision
        - Finish prophecy in detail

### The Conditional Nature of the Prophecy

- **The Time Limit for Israel**
    - If the prophecy is interpreted as conditional, the 70 weeks represent the specific time allotted to Israel to accomplish certain objectives
    - Failure to meet these objectives would result in a transition of focus from Israel to the Gentiles
- **Potential Markers of the Transition**
    - Some point to events roughly three and a half years after Jesus' ministry as indicators that God was "done" with Israel
    - Two specific events are frequently cited:
        - The stoning of Stephen (Acts 7 and 8)
        - The intense persecution of believers in Jerusalem led by Saul of Tarsus (later the Apostle Paul)
    - While the exact traditional dating for Stephen's stoning is debated, it is often used to align with this prophetic timeline

### Historical Context of the Transition

- **The Dismissal of Pontius Pilate**
    - Pilate served as the procurator of Judea for approximately a decade
    - His tenure ended after a massacre against Samaritans at Mount Gerizim
    - This dismissal created significant upheaval within Judea
- **Aligning the Timeline of Stephen's Stoning**
    - Some scholars associate this period of unrest with the rapid growth of the early Christian movement in Jerusalem
    - If Stephen was stoned roughly 3.5 years after the death and resurrection of Jesus (around AD 33-36), it creates a specific chronological marker
    - **[The Problem]** If this specific window is when God is considered "done" with Israel, it raises questions about the implications for the prophetic timeline

### Early Christian Ministry and the Jewish Diaspora

- **The Priority of Jewish Evangelism**
    - Despite the theological discussions regarding a transition away from Israel, the early church and apostles continued to focus heavily on Jewish people
    - The apostles (including Paul, Barnabas, and Silas) maintained a consistent pattern when traveling through the Roman world (e.g., modern-day Turkey, Cyprus, Greece, and Macedonia)
- **The Synagogue Pattern**
    - Upon entering a new town, the first point of contact was almost always the local synagogue
    - Apostles would often attend Shabbat services for several consecutive weeks
    - **[The Process]** The typical progression was:

        1. Preach to the Jewish community in the synagogue
        2. Lead many Jewish people to faith in Jesus
        3. Face ejection or excommunication from the synagogue (often due to jealous local leaders)
        4. Only after being removed from the synagogue would they move on to preach to the Gentiles

- **Theological Basis**
    - This pattern aligns with the principle expressed in Romans 1: "to the Jew first"

### Theological Inconsistency in the 'Transition' Argument

- **The Persistence of the 'Jew First' Priority**
    - If the 70 weeks had concluded and God had officially moved on to the Gentiles, the early church's focus would logically have shifted entirely.
    - **[The Contradiction]** Paul's writings to the Romans (around AD 50-60) occurred a decade or two after the stoning of Stephen, yet he still maintained the priority of the gospel going to the Jews first.
    - Paul's continued habit of visiting synagogues contradicts the idea that the prophetic timeline for Israel had expired.
- **Evidence from the Book of Acts**
    - The expansion of the gospel shows a gradual, overlapping process rather than an abrupt replacement of Israel by the Gentiles.
    - **Examples of the Overlap:**
        - The Samaritans receiving the gospel (beginning in Acts 8).
        - Peter's ministry to Cornelius (a Roman centurion and 'God-fearer' in Acts 10).
            - Cornelius and his household were worshippers of God but not ethnically Jewish.
            - The descent of the Holy Spirit upon them occurred while Peter was preaching, marking a significant moment in the expansion to Gentiles without negating the ongoing Jewish mission.

### Chronological Inconsistencies in the Expansion to Gentiles

- **The Cornelius Timeline Issue**
    - Some argue the conversion of Cornelius's household (c. AD 40) marks the end of the 70 weeks.
    - **[The Discrepancy]** The internal timeline of the Book of Acts suggests a longer period than the three and a half years required by the prophecy.
    - The sequence of events includes:

        1. The persecution of Saul (Paul) in Tarsus
        2. Saul's conversion on the road to Damascus
        3. Saul spending three years in Arabia (as noted in Galatians)
        4. Saul's return to Damascus and subsequent journey to Jerusalem

    - This sequence implies a timeframe of at least five to six years, rather than the three and a half years typically used to align the prophecy.
- **Overlap of Jewish and Gentile Missions**
    - The expansion of the gospel to the Gentiles did not signify an immediate abandonment of the Jewish people.
    - Even as the gospel moved beyond Judea, the Jewish mission remained a priority.
    - The Council of Jerusalem (Acts 15) clarified that Gentiles do not need to become Jews to follow Jesus, yet this did not stop the active evangelism of the Jewish diaspora.

### Continued Priority of the Jewish People

- **Theological and Doctrinal Consistency**
    - The apostles, in both practice and doctrine, maintained that God had not given up on the Jewish people.
    - Paul's writings in Romans 9-11 emphasize that God's work through Israel remained active.
- **Practical Evidence in the Book of Acts**
    - The expansion to the Gentiles did not result in the abandonment of the Jewish mission, at least prior to AD 70.
    - Even at the conclusion of the Book of Acts, Paul's ministry included engaging with Jewish leaders:
        - While under house arrest, Paul brought Jewish elders from the city to him to explain the gospel.

### Alternative Interpretations of the 70 Weeks

- **The 34 AD Deadline Theory**
    - Some interpretations suggest the 70th week concluded around 34 AD.
    - **[The Critique]** There is no explicit evidence in the narrative of the Book of Acts or the Epistles (including those of James or other apostles) to suggest a specific deadline or a sudden pivot in God's plan at that time.
- **Post-millennial and Millennial Perspectives**
    - Certain groups, primarily within the Reformed post-millennial camp and some millennial views, believe that the vast majority of Bible prophecy was fulfilled within the first century.
    - **[The 'Mystery' Argument]** Some suggest the final three and a half years of the prophecy are distinct or "detached" from the first 69 weeks.
        - This view posits that Jesus' death and resurrection brought an end to the sacrificial system (the "mystery"), even if he did not physically continue his earthly ministry in the way expected by the timeline.

### The 70th Week and the First Jewish-Roman War

- **Chronological Alignment**
    - The First Jewish-Roman War (66–70 AD) provides a three-to-four-year window that some use to align with the final stages of the prophecy.
    - This period is often compared to the three years of Jesus' ministry/misery.
- **The 'Gap' Requirement**
    - To interpret the period of 66–70 AD as the final three and a half years of the 70th week, one must accept a chronological gap between the 69th and 70th weeks.
    - **[The Problem]** The narrative of the 70 weeks prophecy does not explicitly indicate a gap between the 69th and 70th weeks.
        - There is significant descriptive language in the transition between Daniel 9:69 and 9:70 that suggests a continuous sequence rather than a disconnected interval.

### Prophetic Foreshortening

- Also referred to as a "prophetic telescope"
- **[Definition]** The idea that events in a prophetic timeline can have significant gaps of many centuries or millennia between them, yet appear compressed together
- **[Purpose]** This compression often revolves around the two comings of Jesus
    - For example, the first coming and the second coming are linked in prophetic literature despite the vast time separating them
    - Isaiah 9:6-7 is cited as a key example of this principle

### Isaiah 61 and Prophetic Compression

![Isaiah 61:1-2 text](hover-notes-images/screenshot-01M57NJDZYR1Y6K0STAKDGZ8YX.png)
[00:32:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-B5EA2SRNro&list=PLU8vICW5zFGo&index=5&t=1935s)

- Isaiah 61:1-2 contains two distinct events joined by a conjunction within the same sentence
    - Event 1: Proclaiming the year of the Lord's favor
    - Event 2: The day of the vengeance of our God
- **[The Significance]** These two events are separated by approximately 2,000 years, yet they are presented as part of a single continuous thought
    - The "year of the Lord's favor" was fulfilled during Jesus's first coming
    - The "day of vengeance" refers to his return

### The Principle of Prophetic Telescoping

- **[Definition]** A prophetic principle where the Spirit allows prophets to view events across history as being much closer together than they actually are
    - This is not "forcing" something into the text that isn't there
    - It is a specific way the prophets could look down the timeline via a vision from the Spirit
- **The Star Analogy**
    - Stars in the night sky appear to be close together to the naked eye
    - Under higher magnification, it becomes clear they are actually millions of light-years apart
    - Prophetic vision functions similarly, compressing vast distances of time into a single view
